How I work

Before the crew arrives

The design decisions come first.

Every project starts with the same instructional design work, and that part is mine: finding the real gap, deciding what people need to do, and planning how I'll know it worked. The AI crew joins once the plan is clear, to help me build it faster.

01 · Analyze

Find the real performance gap: a season I coached, a lesson I observed alongside a teacher, a regulation I read line by line.

02 · Design

Turn it into behaviors, measurable objectives and an assessment plan, using action mapping, ADDIE and UDL.

03 · Prototype

Storyboard and prototype in Figma, Rise 360 or HTML, then walk the flow the way a learner would.

04 · Build & revise

Direct the AI crew to speed up the build, then review every take and revise from feedback.

Scene 2 · Why a director

Directing a crew is designing for learning.

A good director doesn't do every job. They make the vision clear, give each person a role they can own, cue them at the right moment, and check the take before moving on. That's the same work as designing training that moves a whole team toward one outcome. To direct the crew, I have to understand each job well enough to teach it, and that's exactly what instructional design asks of me.

Scene 3 · The problem

The idea isn't the bottleneck. Everything around it is.

Good scenario design is slow, and the idea is rarely the hard part. The time goes to everything around it: turning lived experience into clear behaviors, drafting branches, making dozens of consistent images, catching mismatches across screens, and keeping the case study up to date. Done by hand, that work crowds out the judgment calls that decide whether a scenario actually lands for a learner.

Scene 6 · Rules on set

Six rules every production follows.

  1. No invented numbers

    Every claim is checked against my real facts. If I haven't measured it, it doesn't go on the page.

  2. Nothing ships without a playthrough

    I walk every branch as a learner would before calling a module done, and fix what feels off.

  3. Real people stay protected

    Jersey numbers stand in for student-athletes, AI scenes use no real faces from my teams, and images carry no brand logos.

  4. Every module gets an evaluation plan

    Each project names what success looks like at all four Kirkpatrick levels before I call it done.

  5. The style guide is the standard

    Colors, type, buttons and spacing come from one style guide, so every screen matches without guesswork.

  6. Accessibility is part of done

    Readable sizes, alt text and a reduced-motion setting are built in from the start, not added at the end.

Final cut

The final cut is always mine.

AI doesn't decide what's true, what's fair to a learner, or how a moment should feel. My crew speeds up the busywork so I can spend my time on the parts only a designer should own. I'm open about where AI helps, because being clear about that line is part of designing responsibly.
